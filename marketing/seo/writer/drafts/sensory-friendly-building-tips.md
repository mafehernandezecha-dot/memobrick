# Sensory-Friendly Building Tips

A sensory-friendly brick build is mostly about control: short sessions, one row at a time, colors sorted before you start, a quiet space with good light, and a clear place to stop. These tips are for autistic kids and adults, their families, and anyone who likes a calm, predictable way to make something.

Everyone's senses are different. A sound one person loves can be too much for someone else, so treat this as a menu, not a rulebook. For background on why many people find this kind of building calm, and what the research does and does not say, see our main guide to [brick mosaics as a calm, shared activity](https://mymemobrick.com/blogs/news/brick-mosaic-calm-activity-autistic-kids-adults).

## Keep sessions short

### Decide the length before you start

Pick a clear amount: 10 minutes, 15 minutes, five rows, or one baseplate. Knowing the end point from the start makes it easier to begin.

### Use a visual timer if it helps

A sand timer or a timer with a visible countdown shows how much time is left without anyone needing to ask.

### Stop while it still feels good

End at the next stopping point, before anyone is tired or frustrated. The mosaic will be there tomorrow.

## Build one row at a time

### Why rows work well

A mosaic is a grid, so a row is a natural small task. A 16×16 mosaic is 50 rows of 50 bricks on one baseplate. "Finish this row" is a clear, countable goal.

### Keep your place

Lay a ruler or a strip of paper on the printed color map, just under the row you are building. Move it down when the row is done. Some people tick off finished rows with a pencil.

### Use baseplates as bigger stopping points

Sizes from 20×20 up are made of several baseplates joined together. The 20×20 has four. Finishing one baseplate is a good point to take a longer break or stop for the day.

## Sort colors first

### Start from the labeled bags

The bricks come in small bags labeled by color, with a color name and number on each bag. That already does a lot of the sorting for you.

### Open only what you need

Opening every bag at once can mean a table full of loose bricks. Try opening only the colors in the current row or section, and keep the rest sealed.

### Give each color its own place

A muffin tin, an ice cube tray, or small bowls keep colors apart and within reach. Trays with raised edges stop bricks from sliding off the table.

### Make sorting its own job

For some people, sorting is the most enjoyable part. It can be a separate session, or a separate role if you build together. Our guide to [building together as a reader, sorter and builder](https://mymemobrick.com/blogs/news/build-together-reader-sorter-builder) explains the roles.

## Create a quiet space

### Choose a calm spot

A table away from the TV, a busy kitchen or a hallway helps. A corner with a wall behind the builder can feel more settled.

### Sound

Some builders like silence. Others prefer headphones, ear defenders, familiar music, or a show in the background. Let the builder decide.

### Touch and comfort

A comfortable chair, the right table height, and clothes that feel good matter during a long sit. A soft mat or cloth under the baseplate can reduce the small clicking sound of bricks on a hard table, and stops dropped bricks from bouncing.

## Get the lighting right

### Even, steady light

Similar colors are easier to tell apart in good light. Daylight from a window or a steady desk lamp aimed at the baseplate works well.

### Avoid glare and flicker

Point the lamp so it does not reflect straight into the builder's eyes. If a light flickers or hums, change the bulb or the spot.

### Check colors in the same light

If two shades look alike, compare them next to each other under the same lamp, and check the label on the bag.

## Plan stopping points and pauses

### Agree on how a session ends

Some families use the same short routine each time: finish the row, put the lid on the color trays, take a progress photo. A predictable ending can make stopping easier.

### Protect the work in progress

Leave the baseplate flat in a safe place where it will not be bumped. Cover the color trays, or pour each color back into its bag.

### It is okay to skip a day

There is no deadline unless you set one. Long breaks are fine.

## Celebrate progress

### Make progress visible

Take a photo after every session and look at them in a row, or color in finished rows on a copy of the map. Watching the picture appear is part of what makes the build satisfying.

### Celebrate in the builder's way

Some people love a big reaction. Others prefer a quiet thumbs up, or nothing at all. Ask, or follow their lead.

### Finish with a plan for the mosaic

Decide together where it will go: a wall, a shelf, or a desk. Frames are sold separately if you want one.

## Small-parts safety

The kit contains many small 1×1 bricks.

- It is not suitable for young children or for anyone who puts things in their mouth.
- Keep bags and loose bricks out of reach of small children and pets.
- Build at a table, not on the floor, so bricks are easier to keep track of.
- Do a quick check for loose bricks at the end of each session.

## FAQ

### How long should a building session be?

As long as feels good for the builder. A good start is 10 to 15 minutes or a set number of rows. Decide before you start, and stop at the next stopping point.

### How do I keep my place on the map?

Lay a ruler or strip of paper under the row you are building and move it down as you go. Ticking off finished rows also helps.

### Do the bricks come sorted?

Yes. They come in small bags labeled by color, with a color name and number on each bag.

### Is building with bricks noisy?

Pressing bricks makes a small clicking sound. A soft mat under the baseplate and headphones or ear defenders can help if sound is a concern.

### What size is easiest to start with?

The 16×16 has 2,500 bricks on one baseplate and takes about 2.5 hours in total, so it splits easily into short sessions.

### Is it safe for young children?

No. The kit has many small bricks and is not for young children or anyone who puts things in their mouth.
