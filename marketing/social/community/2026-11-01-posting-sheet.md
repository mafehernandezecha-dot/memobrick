How a photo becomes a picture made of tiny bricks, in 4 steps 🧱

I'm Eden, and I run Memobrick, a small company in Miami that makes photo brick mosaic kits. Crafters always ask how it works, so here's the behind-the-scenes version:

1. Pick a photo. Close-ups in good light turn out best.
2. The photo becomes a grid. Every square is one 1×1 brick in one of 40+ colors.
3. The bricks get sorted into small bags, each labeled with its color name and number (that's them in the picture).
4. You build from a printed color map, pressing each brick onto a white baseplate, row by row, until the picture appears.

The smallest one, 16×16 inches, is 2,500 bricks and about 2.5 hours of building. The big ones join several baseplates together.

For the cross-stitch, diamond painting and perler bead people here: how does that compare to what you make? I'd love to hear what you think.

**Post text:**

A hands-on idea for teaching coordinates and grids, in case it's useful to anyone planning lessons.

A brick mosaic is basically a giant coordinate grid. Each spot on the baseplate has a row and a column, and the color map says which color goes where. Kids practice reading a grid ("row 12, column 7: orange"), counting, sorting by color, and following a pattern, and at the end a picture shows up, which is a nice reward for careful work.

Full disclosure, I'm Eden and I run Memobrick, which makes photo mosaic kits, so I'm biased. You can do the same idea with graph paper and colored pencils: draw a simple picture on a 20×20 grid, write the coordinates, and have a sibling rebuild it from the list.

One safety note if you use bricks: they're small, so keep them away from little ones who put things in their mouths.

What are your favorite hands-on ways to teach grids or graphing?

Hi Lakewood Ranch dog people! 🐕 I'm Eden, from Memobrick, a small Miami business that turns pet photos into brick mosaics. I look at a lot of dog photos, so here are my tips for getting a great one in our Florida sun:

1. Skip midday. Early morning or the hour before sunset gives soft light and no squinting.
2. Find open shade, like a porch or under a tree, so the face isn't half in shadow.
3. Get down to their eye level.
4. Hold a treat right above your phone to get the ears up and the eyes on you.
5. Choose a background that's a different color from their fur.

These work for any photo, not just a mosaic like this Doberman. What's your best trick for getting your dog to sit still for a picture?
